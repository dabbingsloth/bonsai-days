# Bonsai Days · 盆栽の日々

A short web game about patience. You visit **Haruki-sensei**, an elderly bonsai keeper in Ōmiya, Saitama, who gives you a seedling to look after on his windowsill. Over fifteen days you water it, prune it and shape it, while he and his granddaughter **Aoi** tell you the history of bonsai, from Chinese *penjing* to the Edo period, the 1923 earthquake, the Hiroshima survivor pine, and bonsai going global.

Real bonsai takes years, space and money, and the tree can die. Most people only ever look at one. *Bonsai Days* turns that slow daily care into something you can finish in about 20 minutes and safely fail at, and it teaches some culture along the way.

**▶ Play it: https://dabbingsloth.github.io/bonsai-days/**

## How it plays

- **Choose a tree:** Japanese Black Pine, Japanese Maple, or Shimpaku Juniper.
- **15 days.** Each day is a short visual-novel scene, then **one hands-on moment**, then time passes on its own (morning → evening, and the tree grows in a short time-lapse). There is no "Next Day" button and there are no stat meters.
  - **Water:** hold to pour and let go when the gauge reaches the target zone. Overfilling just means letting the pot drain and trying again.
  - **Prune:** tap the glowing shoots that break the tree's outline.
  - **Choices that change the tree:** sun placement, trunk style (formal upright / informal upright / windswept), pot, and the final branch sweep. Each option previews live on your tree before you confirm.
- **Journal:** collects every history note, care tip and design idea you've heard (37 in total).
- **Menu** returns to the tree selection screen. Progress is saved in your browser (`localStorage`) and you can **Continue** later.
- **Epilogue:** your finished tree shown three times, once in each art style.

Every tree grows differently. Each new game uses a random seed, and your choices and pruning change the trunk line, branch angles, foliage density and pot.

## Art direction

The art style changes as the tree grows, and each style matches the period of bonsai history being told. The room, window view, tree, pot, dialogue box, portraits and UI all change together, with a full-screen chapter card at each change.

- The watering can is a traditional copper *jōro*: hammered body, riveted seams, two handles, a long spout and a perforated rose that sprinkles a fine shower.

| Days | Chapter | Style | Story |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1–5 | I · The Woodblock Days | Ukiyo-e woodblock: flat colour, bokashi gradients, cream paper grain, thin ink outlines | Penjing, arrival in Japan, Edo period |
| 6–10 | II · Ink & Motion | Manga: black-and-white ink with bold outlines on the tree and pot, lighter grey lines for the background, flat grey tones, hand-drawn hatching, speed lines and speech bubbles. In the story this is Aoi's sketchbook | Meiji, 1923 earthquake, Ōmiya village, design ideas, Yamaki Pine, 1976 gift |
| 11–15 | III · Light & Pixels | Modern digital painting: soft gradients, glow, glass UI, light particles. In the story this is Aoi's tablet | Bonsai goes global, museum, passing it on |

- The title and selection screens take inspiration from Hokusai's woodblock prints: indigo waves, flat colour, cream paper and a vertical title cartouche. **Every image is original and drawn in code** (SVG generated by JavaScript). There are no image files, and nothing is traced or copied.
- The growing screen is a wooden windowsill in a Japanese house, with shoji screens, a hanging scroll (一期一会, "one time, one meeting") and a window onto a mountain, bamboo and a stone lantern. The sky moves from morning to evening every day, and the lantern lights at dusk.
- The manga chapter uses flat grey tones instead of fine dot screentone, because small dot patterns shimmer on screens and make busy scenes hard to read. Line weight separates the layers: thin grey ink for the view outside, black ink for the room, bold black for the tree and pot.
- The bonsai is procedural: a tapered trunk that grows up out of the soil with a lit side and a shadow side, species bark (cracked plates on the pine, smooth streaks on the maple, twisting fibres on the juniper), surface roots (nebari), tufted moss on gritty soil, and angular, pruned-looking branches with fine twigs ending in layered foliage pads whose outlines are made of individual leaves. Leaves use the real shape for each species: 7-lobed toothed maple leaves with veins, pine needle fascicles with spring "candles", and scale-like juniper sprays. The juniper also gets silver deadwood (shari and jin).
- Haruki-sensei and Aoi are original characters.

## Run it locally

No build step and no dependencies. It's plain HTML, CSS and JavaScript.

- **Quickest:** double-click `index.html`.
- **Or serve the folder** (closer to how GitHub Pages works):
  ```bash
  python -m http.server 8000
  # then open http://localhost:8000
  ```
  or `npx serve .`

Fonts load from Google Fonts. Offline, the game still works with fallback fonts.

## Project structure

```
index.html        screens and HUD markup
css/style.css     layout + the three era themes (body.era-1 / era-2 / era-3)
js/util.js        seeded random numbers, colour mixing, SVG path helpers
js/defs.js        shared SVG definitions: leaf shapes per era, screentones, filters
js/tree.js        the procedural bonsai, pots and moss
js/scene.js       the room and window view, time of day
js/art.js         title print, selection prints, character portraits, chapter cards
js/story.js       all dialogue, day order, journal entries   <- edit the writing here
js/game.js        game flow: dialogue, watering/pruning/choices, saving, epilogue
```

To change the story, edit `js/story.js`. Each day has `intro` lines, one `act` (water / prune / choice) and `after` lines. A line with `j: 'some-id'` unlocks that Journal entry.

## History facts used in the game: please verify

> ⚠️ **These facts were written with AI assistance. Verify each one against your own sources before citing them in coursework.** Dates and details about early bonsai history in particular vary between sources. The characters (Haruki-sensei, Aoi) and their personal stories are fiction.

1. Bonsai grew out of Chinese **penjing** (盆景, "tray scenery"), miniature landscapes with trees and stones in a container.
2. A wall painting in the **tomb of Prince Zhanghuai** (Tang dynasty, **706 CE**) shows attendants carrying miniature landscapes/plants. It is often cited as an early depiction.
3. Potted trees reached **Japan** through contact with China. Picture scrolls from the **Kamakura period / 1300s** show potted trees displayed on verandas.
4. **Buddhist monks, including Zen monks,** are often credited with spreading the art in Japan.
5. The Noh play **Hachi-no-ki** ("The Potted Trees"): a poor samurai burns his three potted trees (**plum, cherry, pine**) to warm a travelling monk, who is really the regent **Hōjō Tokiyori**.
6. The **Edo period (1603–1868)** brought more than 250 years of peace. Potted plants became popular with samurai and townspeople, with plant fairs, gardening books and woodblock prints of potted plants.
7. The word **"bonsai" (盆栽, "tray planting")** came into common use in the **19th century**. Earlier, potted trees were often called *hachi-no-ki*.
8. A Japanese white pine in the **Imperial collection** is said to have been cared for by **Tokugawa Iemitsu (1604–1651)**, the third Tokugawa shōgun.
9. After **1868 (Meiji era)**, bonsai became a pastime of Japan's political and business elite, and public exhibitions became more common.
10. The **Great Kantō Earthquake** struck on **1 September 1923**, followed by fires that devastated Tokyo and Yokohama.
11. Afterwards, bonsai growers from Tokyo moved to **Ōmiya** (now part of Saitama City) for space, clean air, water and soil. The **Ōmiya Bonsai Village** formed in the **mid-1920s** (often given as 1925).
12. Early bonsai pots were **imported from China**. Japanese kilns such as **Tokoname** later became well known for bonsai pots.
13. The **Yamaki Pine**, a Japanese white pine **in training since at least 1625**, survived the **atomic bombing of Hiroshima on 6 August 1945** at the Yamaki family nursery about **3 km (2 miles)** from the hypocentre, sheltered by the nursery walls. The family survived.
14. For the **1976 U.S. Bicentennial**, the **Nippon Bonsai Association** gave **53 bonsai** to the United States. They became the foundation of the **National Bonsai & Penjing Museum** at the **U.S. National Arboretum**, Washington, D.C.
15. **Masaru Yamaki** donated the pine in 1976. Its survival story became widely known in **2001**, when his **grandsons** visited the tree.
16. **John Yoshio Naka (1914–2004)**, a Japanese-American teacher in California, helped spread bonsai internationally. His juniper forest **"Goshin"** is at the National Bonsai & Penjing Museum.
17. The **first World Bonsai Convention** was held in **Ōmiya in 1989**. The **8th World Bonsai Convention** was held in **Saitama in 2017**.
18. The **Ōmiya Bonsai Art Museum** (Saitama City) opened in **2010** and has been described as the first public bonsai museum.
19. Design vocabulary: **chokkan** (formal upright), **moyogi** (informal upright), **fukinagashi** (windswept), **nebari** (surface roots), taper, asymmetry/triangle outline, **ma** (negative space), **wabi-sabi**, and **tokonoma** displays with a hanging scroll and an accent plant. Also the guideline of **unglazed pots for conifers, glazed pots for deciduous/flowering trees**.

**Care tips used in the game** (general guidance; check a horticultural source for your climate):
water when the soil surface starts to dry and water until it drains from the holes; pines, maples and junipers are **outdoor trees** that need a winter rest; black pine and juniper like full sun, while Japanese maple likes morning sun and protection from hot afternoon sun; prune in small steps and don't remove too much foliage at once; repot young trees every few years in early spring; remove training wire before it cuts into the bark; trim junipers with scissors inside the foliage rather than shearing them flat.
